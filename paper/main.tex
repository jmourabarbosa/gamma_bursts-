\begin{abstract}
Working memory is classically attributed to persistent spiking sustained by slow NMDA receptor currents. This view has been challenged by large-scale prefrontal recordings in which the remembered item is decoded only intermittently on single trials, suggesting that memories survive silent periods through synaptic facilitation. Here we show that intermittent coding emerges from a canonical attractor network with fixed synapses and a fluctuating, attention-like common input. Brief drops in attention silence the network, but NMDA currents can bridge each silence. Fitted to monkey prefrontal recordings, the model reproduces the number and duration of silent states and, without refitting, predicts how they change with the decoded neurons. We found that silent states are more frequent in less informative samples, validating this prediction and suggesting that measured silences overestimate how often memory leaves spiking activity. Unlike facilitation, the NMDA trace also predicts memory loss when silences outlast it. In line with this, monkeys made more errors when silent states were longer and their performance pointed to a trace of $\sim$120~ms, matching NMDA kinetics rather than the long time scale of facilitation. Intermittent coding thus reflects persistent activity bridged by NMDA currents.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two main mechanisms have been proposed to hold information in working memory. In persistent-activity models, recurrent excitation keeps a cue-selective population firing through the delay, and slow NMDA receptor currents make this activity stable \citep{amit1997,wang1999,wang2001,compte2000}. Consistent with these models, blocking NMDA receptors markedly reduces persistent firing in monkey prefrontal cortex \citep{wangm2013}. In activity-silent models, the memory is instead stored in short-term synaptic facilitation and needs no ongoing spiking during maintenance \citep{mongillo2008,stokes2015,lundqvist2016,mi2017,miller2018,masse2019,panichello2024}, and hidden memories can be reactivated after periods without decodable activity \citep{rose2016,wolff2017}. Notably, the two accounts rely on traces with very different time scales. NMDA receptor currents decay on a time scale of hundreds of milliseconds \citep{vicini1998,wangh2008}, and models of persistent activity typically use about 100~ms \citep{wang1999,compte2000}. On the other hand, facilitation at excitatory synapses in prefrontal cortex decays on a time scale of the order of a second \citep{thomson1997,markram1998,hempel2000,wangy2006,mongillo2008,mi2017}, and activity-silent models typically use 1.5~s \citep{mongillo2008}. Synaptic augmentation, which lasts tens of seconds, has been proposed to bridge even longer silences \citep{mongillo2025}.

Large-scale, intracranial recordings now make it possible to test these accounts on single trials. Recent studies have shown that working-memory information in prefrontal cortex fluctuates within a trial \citep{panichello2024,tao2024,mozumder2026}. \citet{panichello2024} found that decoding alternates between \emph{On states} (when the cue can be read out) and \emph{Off states} (when it cannot) and concluded that synaptic changes carry the memory through \emph{Off states}. \citet{tao2024} found rare periods of 43--414~ms in which all simultaneously recorded selective neurons were silent for longer than expected by chance, and concluded that short-term plasticity is needed in a very small fraction of trials. Finally, \citet{mozumder2026} found that persistent activity is asynchronous across neurons, with no population silences beyond chance. In their recordings, decoding \emph{Off states} came with reduced but not abolished tuning and were synchronized between prefrontal and parietal cortex, and the authors interpreted them as fluctuations in the fidelity of a persistent code.

Here, we ask whether a single persistent-activity mechanism can account for these observations and propose that intermittent decoding does not require short-term plasticity and is in fact consistent with the NMDA account. In our model, the memory is held in persistent activity supported by recurrent attractors \citep{wang2002,wong2006}. However, fluctuations in a common input, which we interpret as a fluctuating attentional signal, briefly drop and silence the network. During each silence slow NMDA currents carry the memory across, so the cued population resumes firing when the attentional signal returns. We read out from the model with the same decoding and \emph{On/Off} criteria as \citet{panichello2024} and fit how often, for how long and how deeply attention lapses to the \emph{On/Off} statistics of these recordings. The model reproduces quantitatively the number and length of \emph{On and Off states} while keeping the memory. Our reanalyses of the recordings showed that \emph{Off states} are more frequent when fewer (or less selective) neurons are decoded, so their frequency partly reflects how well the recorded sample reads out the memory. Without refitting, the attractor model predicts this dependence for decoded populations it was never fitted to. Intermittent decoding is therefore compatible with persistent activity sustained by NMDA currents, without short-term plasticity. Finally, unlike a model in which short-term facilitation carries the memory, the NMDA model predicts that errors grow with the time spent \emph{Off}, as we found in the recordings. Fitted to the monkeys' performance, the decline in accuracy with \emph{Off} time points to a trace of about 120~ms, which matches NMDA kinetics rather than facilitation. Previous work took intermittent decoding as evidence that persistent activity is not sufficient for working memory, but our results show that this evidence has a simpler explanation within the standard persistent-activity account.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Input dips produce \emph{Off states} and NMDA carries the memory across them}

The model is a classic two-pool attractor network \citep{wang2002,wong2006}, with two memory pools, cued and uncued, that excite themselves through NMDA currents and compete through shared inhibition (Methods). Without fluctuations in its input, the cued pool stays active after the cue and decoding stays \emph{On} (Fig.~1a, first second after the cue). The key addition to this classic model is a common input to both pools that fluctuates, which we interpret as an attentional signal, since attention is known to wax and wane on sub-second time scales (\citealp{fiebelkorn2019}; see Discussion). With brief dips in this input, both pools fall silent and decoding drops to chance (Fig.~1a, after 1,000~ms). During each dip, the NMDA gating of the cued pool decays with a time constant of 150~ms and stays higher than that of the uncued pool. When the input returns, the pool with more NMDA gating wins the competition. \emph{Off states} therefore do not erase the memory, because NMDA gating carries it across each silence, and the cue is still correctly remembered at the end of the delay.

To compare the model with the data, we turned its activity into a decoding z score: the difference between the cued and uncued pool rates in 100~ms windows, multiplied by a gain (c) and added to noise ($\eta$) whose variance and temporal correlations match those of the decoder output in the data (Methods). Importantly, the gain was not fitted to the \emph{On/Off} statistics. Instead, it was calibrated on a single quantity measured in the data, the typical z value when decoding is high, which we call the decoding strength (Methods). \emph{On and Off states} (red and cyan in Fig.~1a) are then labelled with the thresholds of \citet{panichello2024} (dashed lines in Fig.~1a). Read out this way, the model alternates between \emph{On and Off states} (Fig.~1a, bottom). Both length distributions peak near 100~ms and have long tails (Fig.~1b), qualitatively like those in the recordings \citep{panichello2024}. In the next sections, we ask whether the model also matches the data quantitatively.

\subsection{\emph{On/Off} statistics depend on which neurons are decoded}

So far we considered decoders that use all recorded neurons. We found, however, that \emph{On/Off} statistics depend strongly on which neurons the decoder uses, which we call the decoded population (Fig.~2, black circles). Decoders using the most cue-selective neurons spend much more of the delay \emph{On}, and less \emph{Off}, than decoders using the same numbers of randomly chosen neurons, which also show more \emph{Off states} per trial (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over 25 sessions, $p < 10^{-6}$ for each of 5, 20 and 50 neurons; Fig.~2). The decoded populations differ mainly in decoding strength, which is much higher for the most selective neurons. How often a memory appears \emph{Off} therefore depends partly on how well the recorded neurons read it out. This matters for interpreting \emph{Off states} as evidence for activity-silent memory: a decoder trained on a limited sample of neurons can fall to chance while the memory is still present in the activity of other, potentially unrecorded neurons. The measured fraction of time \emph{Off} is therefore an upper bound on how often the memory leaves spiking activity. We then asked if our model could explain this dependence.

To test this, we fitted the dynamics only to the decoder using all neurons in 25 sessions (Methods). The model matches the data well in \emph{Off states} per trial (3.1 vs 3.3), \emph{Off} median length (110 vs 112~ms), \emph{On states} per trial (1.9 vs 2.1) and \emph{On} median length (140 vs 134~ms) (Fig.~2, grey band). We then kept the dynamics fixed and, for each of the six other decoded populations, changed only the read-out without refitting any parameters. Its gain was calibrated on that population's decoding strength and its noise on that population's pre-cue z (Methods). The model thus predicts quantitatively the shift between selective and random neurons (Fig.~2; Supplementary Fig.~1).

\subsection{Errors increase with time \emph{Off}, as expected from an NMDA trace}

So far, we analysed correct trials only, but error trials can help distinguish between the two accounts of working memory. NMDA currents and short-term synaptic facilitation can both carry a memory across brief \emph{Off states}, but on very different time scales. In our model, NMDA gating decays with a time constant of 150~ms, whereas facilitation decays on a time scale of the order of a second \citep{wangy2006,mongillo2008}, comparable to the length of the whole memory period (the delay ended 1.4--1.6~s after cue onset). To see what each mechanism predicts, we compared our model with a version in which facilitation replaces NMDA gating (STP model; Methods). Fitted in the same way, it reproduced the \emph{On/Off} statistics about as well as the NMDA model (Supplementary Fig.~2). We then silenced each model with a single dip of the common input of increasing duration (single-dip test; Methods). The NMDA model held the memory for silences of up to 350~ms and lost it for every silence from 400~ms on, because the NMDA trace had decayed too far for the cued pool to restart (about 2.5 $\tau_N$), whereas the STP model held the memory for silences as long as 1~s (Fig.~3a). The NMDA account therefore predicts that errors become more frequent when the memory stays \emph{Off} for long, whereas facilitation predicts no such relation for silences shorter than its own decay, which here spans the whole delay.

We tested this prediction by comparing \emph{On/Off} statistics between correct and error trials (Methods). Within sessions, the odds of an error increased with the total \emph{Off} time in the delay and with the mean length of \emph{Off states}, and decreased with the total \emph{On} time, but did not depend on the number of \emph{Off states} (Fig.~3b). Errors thus depended on how long the memory was \emph{Off}, not on how often it went \emph{Off}: frequent \emph{Off states} are harmless as long as each is brief. The effect was clear in monkeys H and J (total \emph{Off} time, $\beta$ = 0.48, $p =$ 1$\times10^{-4}$, and $\beta$ = 0.52, $p =$ 0.005; logistic mixed model, Methods) but weak in monkey A ($\beta$ = 0.10, $p =$ 0.13).

Finally, we asked which time constant of the trace best explains how performance falls with \emph{Off} time. We varied $\tau_N$ in the fitted NMDA model, keeping the dip statistics and all other parameters unchanged, and simulated each version with the same random dips, read out as the data. In these simulations, the memory is lost when a single \emph{Off} period outlasts the NMDA trace and the cued pool cannot restart (with $\tau_N$ = 150~ms, single dips longer than about 375~ms; Fig.~3a), whereas long \emph{Off} times made of several brief \emph{Off states} are mostly bridged. We linked the probability that the memory was held to the probability of a correct response, assuming that the monkeys guess among the 8 locations after a memory loss and that other errors do not depend on \emph{Off} time (Methods). With the best-fitting $\tau_N$ of 123~ms, the model reproduced the decline of the pooled fraction correct (Fig.~3c). Resampling sessions gave a 95\% confidence interval of 101--190~ms (Fig.~3d). The estimate also hardly depended on what the monkeys do after losing the memory. Assuming that they are always wrong, or guess correctly up to half of the time, gave 102--129~ms (Methods). The decline of performance with \emph{Off} time thus calls for a trace of about 120~ms, close to measured NMDA receptor kinetics and to the 150~ms assumed in the fitted model, and far shorter than the time scale of facilitation, of the order of a second \citep{thomson1997,markram1998,hempel2000,wangy2006,mongillo2008}.

\section{Discussion}

Recent large-scale recordings found that prefrontal decoding of a remembered item is intermittent on single trials \citep{panichello2024,tao2024,mozumder2026}, suggesting that memories survive silent periods through synaptic facilitation \citep{mongillo2008,stokes2015,lundqvist2016,mi2017,miller2018,masse2019,panichello2024}. We showed that the same \emph{On/Off} statistics arise in a conventional attractor network with fixed synapses. In the model, \emph{Off states} occur when a common input briefly drops, and the memory survives each silence because NMDA receptor currents decay slowly enough to bias which pool restarts. Fitted to the decoder trained on the empirical data, the model reproduced the number and length of \emph{On and Off states} while keeping the memory. Unlike a model in which short-term facilitation carries the memory, it also predicted that errors grow with the time spent \emph{Off}, which we validated in prefrontal recordings.

In the recordings, \emph{Off states} were more frequent and \emph{On states} rarer when the decoder used random rather than selective neurons. This showed that a decoder falls to chance whenever the memory signal in its sample is weak relative to its noise, not only when the memory is absent from the network. The fraction of time \emph{Off} measured from a limited sample is therefore an upper bound on how often the memory leaves spiking activity, and comparisons of \emph{Off-state} statistics across studies or brain areas need to take the decoded population into account. The model captured this shift without refitting by changing only the read-out gain and noise, both calibrated from the data.

Our account is consistent with the other recent recordings of \emph{Off states}. \citet{mozumder2026} found that \emph{Off states} were synchronized between prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex, came with lower firing rates, and showed no obvious rhythmicity. A common input that reaches several areas and fluctuates irregularly, as in our fit, would produce such coordinated, aperiodic \emph{Off states}. \citet{tao2024} likewise found that information is reduced but present between gamma bursts. Our model is compatible with both, because NMDA currents can carry the memory across full silences but are not needed when activity is only reduced. While attention is sustained, the memory is held by persistent activity without any \emph{Off state} (Fig.~1a, first second after the cue).

We interpreted the common input as an attentional signal that is high when attention is deployed on the memorandum and drops during lapses. Two lines of evidence support this interpretation. First, \citet{engel2016} found that spiking in macaque area V4 alternates between \emph{On and Off} episodes that occur synchronously across cortical layers. These states were identified directly from population spiking, not from a decoder. \emph{Off} episodes lasted about 100~ms on average (102~ms during stimulus-driven and 118~ms during spontaneous activity), close to the \emph{Off states} in prefrontal decoding (about 110~ms) and within the time an NMDA trace can bridge. Spatial attention lengthened \emph{On} episodes and made transitions less frequent, and monkeys detected a stimulus change more often when it occurred during an \emph{On} episode than during an \emph{Off} episode (78\% vs 65\%). Second, sustained attention samples the environment rhythmically rather than continuously. In humans, detection of threshold stimuli depends on the phase of ongoing EEG oscillations near 7~Hz \citep{busch2009}, especially at attended locations \citep{busch2010}. When attention is divided between two locations, detection at each fluctuates at about 4~Hz \citep{landau2012}. In monkeys, frontal eye field and lateral intraparietal activity alternates at theta frequencies (3--8~Hz) between periods of enhanced perception and periods in which attention is more likely to shift \citep{fiebelkorn2018}, and human intracranial recordings show similar theta-rhythmic sampling in frontoparietal cortex \citep{helfrich2018}. Because attention and working memory share prefrontal mechanisms \citep{awh2001,panichello2021}, lapses of attention could plausibly reach the memory network. In the fitted model, the attention signal recovers about five times per second, or one cycle every $\sim$200~ms. This is in the low theta range reported for attentional sampling \citep{landau2012,fiebelkorn2018,helfrich2018} and only slightly slower than the $\sim$7~Hz phase effects on detection \citep{busch2009,busch2010}. However, the fitted signal was not periodic. A direct test would be to ask whether \emph{Off-state} onsets lock to the phase of theta activity in the prefrontal local field potential, or whether probes presented during \emph{Off states} are detected less often.

In our model, the memory is carried across each silence by NMDA gating that decays with a time constant of 150~ms and reflects recent firing rather than plasticity. This time constant lies within the measured decay times of NMDA receptor currents, from about 50~ms for receptors containing the GluN2A subunit to several hundred milliseconds for those containing the GluN2B (NR2B) subunit \citep{vicini1998}, and GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors are required for persistent firing in primate prefrontal cortex \citep{wangm2013}. \emph{Off states} last about 100~ms, within the time an NMDA trace can bridge, so no longer-lasting mechanism is needed to explain them. The behavioural data point the same way. A memory held by facilitation decaying on a time scale of the order of a second should hardly be affected by a few hundred more milliseconds \emph{Off}, as the STP model showed. We found that errors increased with the time spent \emph{Off} and with the length of \emph{Off states}, but not with their number (Fig.~3). This is expected from a trace that bridges brief silences but not long ones. Fitted to the monkeys' performance, the time constant of this trace was about 120~ms (95\% CI 101--190~ms), close to measured NMDA receptor kinetics and far shorter than that of facilitation. Activity-silent mechanisms may still matter on longer time scales, for example to carry information between trials, when prefrontal memory traces are reactivated before the next stimulus \citep{barbosa2020}. Notably, this between-trial trace is not a voluntary memory. It does not require attention and shows up as involuntary serial biases in behaviour, rather than as the information the subject actively maintains \citep{kiyonaga2017,pascucci2023}. Persistent activity and slower silent traces are therefore not mutually exclusive.

In sum, intermittent decoding of working memory is expected from an attractor network with slow NMDA currents and a fluctuating, attention-like input. Its statistics depend on how well the recorded neurons read out the memory, and errors increase with the time spent \emph{Off}, as expected from the limited duration of the NMDA trace. These results reconcile intermittent rate coding with persistent-activity accounts identify a trace of about 120~ms that bridges \emph{Off states}, and link \emph{Off states} to the rhythmic but irregular nature of attentional sampling.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Rate model}

The network is the reduced two-pool attractor model of \citet{wong2006}, with the changes described below.
Two excitatory memory pools, $i \in \{\cued, \uncued\}$, have rates $r_i$ and NMDA gating $s_i$.
They share an inhibitory population $r_I$ and receive a feed-forward inhibition $q$ that follows the common input $m(t)$ with a lag.
Parameters in bold were free during fitting; all others were fixed.
\begin{align}
  \tau_r \frac{dr_i}{dt} &= -r_i + \varphi(x_i), \\
  x_i &= \bm{w_s} s_i + w_a r_i - w_I r_I + I_0\, m(t) - w_{\mathit{ff}}\, q + \mathrm{cue}_i + \bm{\sigma} \xi_i, \\
  \tau_N \frac{ds_i}{dt} &= -s_i + \beta\, r_i (1 - s_i), \\
  \tau_I \frac{dr_I}{dt} &= -r_I + w_{EI} \left(r_\cued + r_\uncued\right), \\
  \tau_{\mathit{ff}} \frac{dq}{dt} &= -q + m(t).
\end{align}
The transfer function $\varphi$ is a logistic sigmoid, $\varphi(x) = r_{\max} / \bigl(1 + \exp(-(x - 1)/\kappa)\bigr)$, with maximum rate $r_{\max}$, threshold 1 and slope $\kappa$, and $\xi_i$ are independent Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noises ($\tau_\xi = 10$~ms).
Fixed parameters: $r_{\max} = 150$, $\kappa = 0.06$, $w_a = 0.0141$, $\beta = 0.02$, $w_I = 0.04$, $w_{EI} = 0.5$, $w_{\mathit{ff}} = 0.8$, $I_0 = 1.496$; $\tau_r = 10$~ms, $\tau_I = 1$~ms, $\tau_{\mathit{ff}} = 15$~ms, $\tau_N = 150$~ms.
The implementation differs from \citet{wong2006} in three respects: the transfer function is a saturating sigmoid, competition between the pools goes through an explicit inhibitory population, and a lagging feed-forward inhibition is added.
Parameter values could therefore not be taken directly from their study.
We chose them by hand so that each pool is bistable, with a low state near 0~Hz and a high state of about 85~Hz, and so that shared inhibition allows only one pool to be active at a time.
The cue is a 50~ms input of amplitude 1 to the cued pool, after 300~ms of baseline, followed by a 1,600~ms delay.
Equations were integrated with Euler steps of 0.1~ms.
The memory was scored correct when, in the last 100~ms, the NMDA gating of the cued pool exceeded the uncued pool's by more than 0.05.
We scored NMDA gating rather than rates because the network may be silent at the end of the delay, and a strong attentional input, modelling the response period, restarts the pool with more NMDA gating and so recovers the correct response (not shown).

Our main modification to this model is the common input $m(t)$ to both pools, which represents fluctuating attention.
It is 1 except during dips, where it drops to $1 - \bm{\delta}$ with 10~ms ramps.
Dip lengths are gamma-distributed (mean $\bm{\mu_d}$, shape $\bm{k}$), and the gaps between dips are exponential with mean $1/\bm{\lambda_d}$.

\subsection{Data}

We reanalysed the recordings of \citet{panichello2024}: 25 sessions, correct trials only (error trials were used only in the behavioural analysis), all cue locations (13,190 trials; 329 units per session on average).
For each time bin, an L2-regularised logistic decoder ($\lambda = 10{,}000$) was evaluated with approximate leave-one-out and $z$-scored against 30 shuffled label sets per cue pair.
Decoded populations were all neurons, the $k$ most cue-selective neurons (ranked by split-half ANOVA), and $k$ randomly chosen neurons (10 draws, averaged within sessions), with $k = 5$, 20 and 50.
Decoded populations of the same size were compared within sessions with two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the session values (random draws averaged within each session).

\subsection{Behavioural analysis}

To relate \emph{On/Off states} to behaviour, we also labelled error trials (15,468 trials in total, 2,278 errors). Error trials were decoded with decoders fitted on all correct trials of the same cue pair, so they were also out of sample, and their rates were z-scored with the mean and SD of correct trials. For each trial, we computed the total \emph{Off} time and the total \emph{On} time in the delay (500--1,400~ms after cue onset), the number of \emph{Off states} and their mean length (13,962 trials with at least one \emph{Off state}). For each statistic, the probability of an error was modelled with a logistic mixed model, error ${\sim}$ z(stat) + t + t² + monkey + (1 + z(stat) || session), where z(stat) is the statistic z-scored within each session, so that only within-session variation is used, t is the time within the session, and each session has a random intercept and an uncorrelated random slope. Monkey was a fixed effect, as three monkeys are too few to estimate a random effect. Models were fitted with the Laplace approximation and tested with likelihood-ratio tests against the same model without the statistic. For the fit of $\tau_N$, trials were pooled over sessions and binned by \emph{Off} time in 90~ms bins (one tenth of the delay window).

\subsection{STP model}

To compare our model with a short-term plasticity account, we replaced the NMDA gating $s_i$ in the recurrent input by a saturating synaptic activation without a time constant of its own, $S_i = g_i/(1 + g_i)$, with $g_i = \beta r_i u_i/U$, where $u_i$ is Tsodyks--Markram short-term facilitation \citep{mongillo2008,mi2017}:
\begin{equation*}
  \tau_f \frac{du_i}{dt} = U - u_i + \tau_f U (1 - u_i) r_i,
\end{equation*}
with $U = 0.3$ and $\tau_f = 1.5$~s. To simplify fitting and to give the STP model the best chance to fit the data, synaptic depression was switched off: with depression ($\tau_d = 0.3$~s), each re-ignition ended too early and \emph{On states} were too short (112 vs 172~ms in the data). All other parameters were as in the NMDA model, and the STP model was fitted in the same way to the decoder using all neurons, with an additional penalty for population spikes before the cue. Best fit: $\bm{w_s} = 0.69$, $\bm{\lambda_d} = 5.5$/s, $\bm{\mu_d} = 212$~ms, $\bm{k} = 0.70$, $\bm{\delta} = 0.39$, $\bm{\sigma} = 0.005$. Both models matched the \emph{On/Off} statistics about equally well (loss 9.7 for the NMDA model and 10.4 for the STP model, on 2,112 trials) and held the memory in nearly all trials.

\subsection{Simulations of memory against \emph{Off} time}

To compare with the behaviour, each fitted model was then simulated with random dips drawn from its fitted dip statistics (100,000 trials per model); the memory was held in 98.5\% (NMDA model) and 99.99\% (STP model) of these trials. To measure how long each model can hold the memory through a single silence (single-dip test, Fig.~3a), we simulated trials with only one dip of the common input, starting 500~ms after cue onset, with the model's fitted depth and a length from 0 to 1,000~ms in 25~ms steps (300 trials per length, 12,300 trials per model). Fig.~3a shows the fraction of these trials with the memory held against dip duration. Trials were read out with the all-units gain and noise of the fitted model, and the memory was scored as in the NMDA model, using the facilitation $u_i$ instead of the NMDA gating in the STP model. \emph{Off} time was computed as in the data.

\subsection{NMDA time constant fitted to performance}

The fitted NMDA model, with random dips drawn from the fitted dip statistics (the same dips for every $\tau_N$) and the all-units read-out, was simulated with $\tau_N$ from 20 to 1,500~ms (17 values, 30,000 trials each) and all other parameters unchanged, so the \emph{On/Off} statistics drift away from their fitted values as $\tau_N$ changes. For each $\tau_N$, the probability that the memory was held was computed in the ten \emph{Off}-time bins and interpolated in log $\tau_N$. The model was linked to behaviour by P(correct) = $p_0$ $\times$ [P(held) + G $\times$ P(lost)], where G = 1/8 is the chance of a correct guess among the 8 cue locations after the memory is lost, and $p_0$ is the performance when the memory is held, which absorbs errors that do not depend on \emph{Off} time. $\tau_N$ and $p_0$ were fitted by maximum likelihood to all 15,468 trials pooled (best $p_0$ = 0.887). Confidence intervals came from 1,000 bootstraps over sessions (25 sessions drawn with replacement, their trials pooled and refitted). The estimate depended little on G: the best $\tau_N$ was 129, 118 and 102~ms for G = 0, 1/4 and 1/2.

\subsection{Fitting}

Six parameters were fitted with the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy \citep[CMA-ES;][]{hansen2001} to the decoder using all neurons: $\bm{w_s}$, $\bm{\lambda_d}$, $\bm{\mu_d}$, $\bm{k}$, $\bm{\delta}$ and $\bm{\sigma}$.
The NMDA decay time was not fitted but fixed at $\tau_N = 150$~ms, within the range measured in cortex \citep{vicini1998,wangm2013}.
The loss combined the squared scaled errors of six statistics (\emph{On and Off states} per trial, mean lengths and fractions of the delay), the squared Hellinger distance between model and data length distributions (states $\geq 40$~ms), and a penalty when the memory was correct in fewer than 98\% of trials.
Best fit: $\bm{w_s} = 1.30$, $\bm{\lambda_d} = 10.2$/s, $\bm{\mu_d} = 90$~ms, $\bm{k} = 8.7$, $\bm{\delta} = 0.60$, $\bm{\sigma} = 0.006$.

\subsection{Decoding read-out, \emph{On/Off} labelling and statistics}

The decoder $z$ score was modelled as $z(t) = c\, \Delta r(t) + \eta(t)$, where $\Delta r$ is the cued minus uncued rate in 100~ms windows stepped by 10~ms.
The noise $\eta$ has unit variance, the autocorrelation of overlapping windows, and a slow per-trial offset whose variance share was measured from the pre-cue $z$ of the data (0.35 for all neurons, 0.09 for the most selective neurons, 0.11 for random neurons).
The gain $c$ was not fitted: for each decoded population, it was chosen so that the upper mean of a two-component Gaussian mixture of delay $z$ values matched the data's.
We call this upper mean the decoding strength.
States were labelled as in \citet{panichello2024}.
\emph{On states} are runs of $z > 1.96$ whose summed $z$ exceeds the 95th percentile of the same quantity in pure noise.
\emph{Off states} are runs of at least 5 points (50~ms) with $z < 0.84$.
States were counted from 0 to 1,400~ms after cue onset, and fractions of the delay spent \emph{On} or \emph{Off} from 500 to 1,400~ms.
Model statistics were computed from 2,112 simulated trials (792 per evaluation during fitting).
Data statistics are means over the 25 sessions; for random neurons, the 10 draws were first averaged within each session.
