\title{Intermittent rate coding without short-term plasticity}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Working memory is thought to rely either on persistent spiking activity, sustained by slow NMDA receptor currents, or on activity-silent synaptic traces. Recently, population recordings from non-human primate prefrontal cortex showed that the remembered item can be decoded only intermittently on single trials, with Off periods in which decoding falls to chance. These Off periods have been taken as evidence that memories are carried by short-term synaptic plasticity when spiking activity fails to hold them. Here we show that intermittent decoding is also expected from a canonical attractor network without plastic synapses. When a shared input (such as fluctuating attention) briefly drops, the network falls silent and decoding fails. However, NMDA receptor currents, which decay over 150~ms as measured in cortical neurons, bridge the silence and the memory is reinstated when the attentional input returns. Fitted to population recordings from non-human primate prefrontal cortex, this model reproduces quantitatively the number and duration of On and Off states while retaining the memory. In the recordings, we found that Off states are more frequent when fewer or less selective neurons are decoded, so their frequency partly reflects how well the recorded sample reads out the memory. Without refitting, the model predicts this dependence for decoded populations it was never fitted to. Intermittent decoding is therefore compatible with persistent activity and does not, on its own, require activity-silent mechanisms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two main mechanisms have been proposed to hold information in working memory. In persistent-activity models, recurrent excitation keeps a cue-selective population firing through the delay, and slow NMDA receptor currents make this activity stable \citep{amit1997,wang1999,wang2001,compte2000}. In activity-silent models, the memory is instead stored in short-term synaptic facilitation and needs no ongoing spikes for memory maintenance \citep{mongillo2008,stokes2015}.

Large-scale recordings now make it possible to test these accounts on single trials. Recent studies agree that working-memory information in prefrontal cortex fluctuates within a trial, but disagree on what the fluctuations mean. \citet{panichello2024} found that decoding alternates between On states, when the cue can be read out, and Off states, when it cannot, and concluded that synaptic changes carry the memory through Off states. \citet{tao2024} found that information carried by spiking activity is reduced but still present between spike bursts. They also found rare periods of 43–414~ms in which all simultaneously recorded selective neurons were silent for longer than expected by chance, and concluded that short-term plasticity is needed only in a very small fraction of trials. \citet{mozumder2026} found that persistent activity is asynchronous across neurons, with no population silences beyond chance. In their recordings, decoding Off states came with reduced but not abolished tuning and were synchronized between prefrontal and parietal cortex, and the authors interpreted them as fluctuations in the fidelity of a persistent code.

Here, we ask whether a single persistent-activity mechanism can account for these observations and propose that intermittent decoding does not require short-term plasticity and is in fact consistent with the NMDA account. In our model, the memory is held in persistent activity supported by recurrent attractors \citep{wang2002,wong2006}. However, fluctuations in a common input, which we interpret as a fluctuating attentional signal, briefly drops and silences the network. In between silences, slow NMDA currents carry the memory across, so the cued population resumes firing when the attentional signal returns. We read out the model with the same decoding and On/Off criteria as \citet{panichello2024} and fit how often, for how long and how deeply attention lapses to the On/Off statistics of these recordings. The model reproduces quantitatively the number and length of On and Off states without memory loss. Reanalyses of the recordings showed that Off states are more frequent when fewer (or less selective) neurons are decoded, so their frequency partly reflects how well the recorded sample, a tiny fraction of the neurons that hold the memory, reads it out. Without refitting, the attractor model predicts this dependence out of distribution for decoded populations it was never fitted to. Intermittent decoding is therefore compatible with persistent activity sustained by NMDA currents, without short-term plasticity.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Input dips produce Off states and NMDA carries the memory across them}

The model is a classic two-pool attractor network \citep{wang2002,wong2006}, with two memory pools, cued and uncued, that excite themselves through NMDA currents and compete through shared inhibition (Methods). After the cue, the cued pool stays active and decoding stays On for the whole delay (Fig.~1a, first second after the cue). The key addition to this classic model is a common input to both pools that fluctuates, which we interpret as an attentional signal, since attention is known to wax and wane on sub-second time scales \citep[see Discussion]{fiebelkorn2019}. With brief dips in this input, both pools fall silent and decoding drops to chance (Fig.~1a, after 1,000~ms). During each dip, the NMDA gating of the cued pool decays with a time constant of 150~ms and stays higher than that of the uncued pool. When the input returns, lagging feed-forward inhibition gives both pools a brief kick, and the pool with more NMDA gating wins the competition. Off states therefore do not erase the memory and NMDA gating carries it across each silence, ensuring a correct memory of the cue at the end of the delay.

To compare the model with the data, we turned its activity into a decoding $z$ score: the difference between the cued and uncued pool rates in 100~ms windows, multiplied by a gain ($c$) and added to noise ($\eta$) whose variance and temporal correlations match those of the decoder output in the data (Methods). Importantly, the gain was not fitted to the On/Off statistics. Instead, it was calibrated on a single quantity measured in the data, the typical $z$ value when decoding is high, which we call the decoding strength (Methods). On and Off states (red and cyan in Fig.~1a) are then labelled with the same thresholds as in \citet{panichello2024} (red and cyan dashed lines in Fig.~1a). Read out this way, the model alternates between On and Off states (Fig.~1a, bottom). Both length distributions peak near 100~ms and have long tails (Fig.~1b), qualitatively like those in the recordings \citep{panichello2024}. In the next sections, we ask whether the model also matches the data quantitatively, including for decoders built from different sets of neurons.

\subsection{On/Off statistics depend on which neurons are decoded}

So far we considered decoders that use all recorded neurons. We found, however, that On/Off statistics depend strongly on which neurons the decoder uses, which we call the decoded population (Fig.~2, black circles). Decoders using the most cue-selective neurons spend much more of the delay On, and less Off, than decoders using the same numbers of randomly chosen neurons, which also show more Off states per trial. The decoded populations differ mainly in decoding strength, which is much higher for the most selective neurons. How often a memory appears Off therefore depends partly on how well the recorded neurons read it out. This matters for interpreting Off states as evidence for activity-silent memory: a decoder trained on a limited sample of neurons can fall to chance while the memory is still present in the activity of other, potentially unrecorded neurons. The measured fraction of Off states is therefore an upper bound on how often the memory leaves spiking activity. We then asked if our model could explain this dependence.

\subsection{A model fitted to all neurons predicts this dependence}

If Off states come from shared dips in network activity, the same dynamics, read out with a higher or lower decoding strength, should reproduce the change across decoded populations. To test this, we fitted the dynamics only to the decoder using all neurons in 25 sessions (Methods). The model matches the data well in Off states per trial (3.1 vs 3.3), Off median length (110 vs 112~ms), On states per trial (1.9 vs 2.1) and On median length (140 vs 134~ms) (Fig.~2, grey band). We then kept the dynamics fixed and, for each of the six other decoded populations, changed only the read-out without refitting any parameters. Its gain was calibrated on that population's decoding strength and its noise on that population's pre-cue $z$ (Methods). Without any refitting, the model predicts quantitatively the shift between selective and random neurons.

\subsection{More time Off predicts errors}

Our account also makes a behavioural prediction. If the memory survives each Off state only because NMDA gating decays slowly, it should be more fragile when the network spends more time silent, since the gating decays throughout each silence. We therefore asked whether On/Off statistics differ between correct and error trials, which were excluded so far (Methods). Within sessions, the odds of an error increased with the total Off time in the delay and with the mean length of Off states, and decreased with the fraction of the delay On, but did not depend on the number of Off states (Fig.~3a). Accordingly, the fraction of correct trials fell steadily with Off time, from 91\% in trials with almost no Off time to 76\% in trials Off for nearly the whole delay (Fig.~3b). The effect was present in two of the three monkeys (Methods). Errors are thus associated with more time silent, not with more frequent silences, as expected if the memory is occasionally lost when the NMDA trace decays too far during a silence. Because error trials were decoded with decoders trained on correct trials, part of their Off time could also reflect a wrong remembered location rather than a lapse.

\section{Discussion}

Recent large-scale recordings agree that prefrontal decoding of a remembered item is intermittent on single trials, but differ on whether Off states mean that the memory leaves spiking activity and is held in short-term synaptic plasticity \citep{panichello2024,tao2024,mozumder2026}. We showed that the same On/Off statistics arise in a conventional attractor network with fixed synapses. In the model, Off states occur when a common input briefly drops, and the memory survives each silence because NMDA receptor currents decay slowly enough to bias which pool restarts. Fitted to the decoder trained on the empirical data, the model reproduced the number and length of On and Off states and kept the memory in 98\% of trials.

In the recordings, Off states were more frequent and On states rarer when the decoder used random rather than selective neurons. This showed that a decoder falls to chance whenever the memory signal in its sample is weak relative to its noise, not only when the memory is absent from the network. The fraction of time Off measured from a limited sample is therefore an upper bound on how often the memory leaves spiking activity, and comparisons of Off-state statistics across studies or brain areas need to take the decoded population into account. The model captured this shift without refitting by changing only the read-out gain and noise, both calibrated from the data.

Our account is consistent with the other recent recordings of Off states. \citet{mozumder2026} found that Off states were synchronized between prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex, came with lower firing rates, and showed no obvious rhythmicity. A common input that reaches several areas and fluctuates irregularly, as in our fit, would produce such coordinated, aperiodic Off states. They also found that tuning was reduced by about 78\% but not abolished during Off states. \citet{tao2024} likewise found that information is reduced but present between gamma bursts. Our model is compatible with both: NMDA currents carry the memory across full silences, but the model does not need them, since the memory is held by persistent activity without any Off state while attention is sustained (Fig.~1a, first second after the cue).

We interpreted the common input as an attentional signal that is high when attention is deployed on the memorandum and drops during lapses. This links intermittent decoding to evidence that sustained attention samples rhythmically rather than continuously. In humans, detection of threshold stimuli depends on the phase of ongoing EEG oscillations near 7~Hz \citep{busch2009}, especially at attended locations \citep{busch2010}. When attention is divided between two locations, detection at each fluctuates at about 4~Hz, in antiphase between locations \citep{landau2012}. In monkeys, frontal eye field and lateral intraparietal activity alternates at theta frequencies (3–8~Hz) between periods of enhanced perception and periods in which attention is more likely to shift \citep{fiebelkorn2018}, and human intracranial recordings show similar theta-rhythmic sampling in frontoparietal cortex \citep{helfrich2018}. These findings have been unified in a rhythmic theory of attention, in which theta rhythms alternate between sampling and shifting \citep{fiebelkorn2019}. Because attention and working memory share prefrontal mechanisms \citep{awh2001,panichello2021}, lapses of attention could plausibly reach the memory network. In the fitted model, the attention signal recovers about five times per second, or one cycle every ${\sim}$200~ms. This is in the low theta range reported for attentional sampling \citep{landau2012,fiebelkorn2018,helfrich2018} and only slightly slower than the ${\sim}$7~Hz phase effects on detection \citep{busch2009,busch2010}. However, the fitted signal was not periodic. A direct test would be to ask whether Off-state onsets lock to the phase of theta activity in the prefrontal local field potential, or whether probes presented during Off states are detected less often.

In our model, the memory is carried across each silence by NMDA gating that decays within ${\sim}$150~ms and reflects recent firing rather than plasticity. This time constant lies within the measured decay times of NMDA receptor currents, which are slowest for receptors containing the GluN2B (NR2B) subunit \citep{vicini1998}, and GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors are required for persistent firing in primate prefrontal cortex \citep{wangm2013}. Off states last about 100~ms, within the time an NMDA trace can bridge, so no longer-lasting mechanism is needed to explain them. Activity-silent mechanisms may still matter on longer time scales, for example to carry information between trials, when prefrontal memory traces are reactivated before the next stimulus \citep{barbosa2020}. Notably, this between-trial trace is not a voluntary memory. It does not require attention and shows up as involuntary serial biases in behaviour, rather than as the information the subject actively maintains. Importantly, the model restarts the memory with a brief transient: when the common input returns, excitation precedes lagging feed-forward inhibition by ${\sim}$15~ms. Without such a transient, pools silenced by a dip often failed to restart. We implemented it as feed-forward inhibition, a common circuit motif, but recovery of input synapses from depression during a dip would produce a similar onset overshoot. Persistent activity and slower silent traces are therefore not mutually exclusive.

In sum, intermittent decoding of working memory is expected from an attractor network with slow NMDA currents and a fluctuating, attention-like input, and its statistics depend on how well the recorded neurons read out the memory. These results reconcile intermittent rate coding with persistent-activity accounts and link Off states to the rhythmic but irregular nature of attentional sampling.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Rate model}

The network is the reduced two-pool attractor model of \citet{wong2006}, with the changes described below.
Two excitatory memory pools, $i \in \{\cued, \uncued\}$, have rates $r_i$ and NMDA gating $s_i$.
They share an inhibitory population $r_I$ and receive a feed-forward inhibition $q$ that follows the common input $m(t)$ with a lag.
Parameters in bold were free during fitting; all others were fixed.
\begin{align}
  \tau_r \frac{dr_i}{dt} &= -r_i + \varphi(x_i), \\
  x_i &= \bm{w_s} s_i + w_a r_i - w_I r_I + I_0\, m(t) - w_{\mathit{ff}}\, q + \mathrm{cue}_i + \bm{\sigma} \xi_i, \\
  \tau_N \frac{ds_i}{dt} &= -s_i + \beta\, r_i (1 - s_i), \\
  \tau_I \frac{dr_I}{dt} &= -r_I + w_{EI} \left(r_\cued + r_\uncued\right), \\
  \tau_{\mathit{ff}} \frac{dq}{dt} &= -q + m(t).
\end{align}
The transfer function $\varphi$ is a logistic sigmoid, $\varphi(x) = r_{\max} / \bigl(1 + \exp(-(x - 1)/\kappa)\bigr)$, with maximum rate $r_{\max}$, threshold 1 and slope $\kappa$, and $\xi_i$ are independent Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noises ($\tau_\xi = 10$~ms).
Fixed parameters: $r_{\max} = 150$, $\kappa = 0.06$, $w_a = 0.0141$, $\beta = 0.02$, $w_I = 0.04$, $w_{EI} = 0.5$, $w_{\mathit{ff}} = 0.8$, $I_0 = 1.496$; $\tau_r = 10$~ms, $\tau_I = 1$~ms, $\tau_{\mathit{ff}} = 15$~ms, $\tau_N = 150$~ms.
The implementation differs from \citet{wong2006} in three respects: the transfer function is a saturating sigmoid, competition between the pools goes through an explicit inhibitory population, and a lagging feed-forward inhibition is added.
Parameter values could therefore not be taken directly from their study.
We chose them by hand so that each pool is bistable, with a low state near 0~Hz and a high state of about 85~Hz, and so that shared inhibition allows only one pool to be active at a time.
The cue is a 50~ms input of amplitude 1 to the cued pool, after 300~ms of baseline, followed by a 1,600~ms delay.
Equations were integrated with Euler steps of 0.1~ms.
The memory was scored correct when, in the last 100~ms, the NMDA gating of the cued pool exceeded the uncued pool's by more than 0.05.
We scored NMDA gating rather than rates because the network may be silent at the end of the delay, but a strong attentional input during the response period can restart the pool with more NMDA gating and recover the correct response with virtually no memory loss (not shown).

Our main modification to this model is the common input $m(t)$ to both pools, which represents fluctuating attention.
It is 1 except during dips, where it drops to $1 - \bm{\delta}$ with 10~ms ramps.
Dip lengths are gamma-distributed (mean $\bm{\mu_d}$, shape $\bm{k}$), and the gaps between dips are exponential with mean $1/\bm{\lambda_d}$.

\subsection{Data}

We reanalysed the recordings of \citet{panichello2024}: 25 sessions, correct trials only (error trials were used only in the behavioural analysis), all cue locations (13,190 trials; 329 units per session on average).
For each time bin, an L2-regularised logistic decoder ($\lambda = 10{,}000$) was evaluated with approximate leave-one-out and $z$-scored against 30 shuffled label sets per cue pair.
Decoded populations were all neurons, the $k$ most cue-selective neurons (ranked by split-half ANOVA), and $k$ randomly chosen neurons (10 draws, averaged within sessions), with $k = 5$, 20 and 50.

\subsection{Behavioural analysis}

To relate On/Off states to behaviour, we also labelled error trials (15,468 trials in total, 2,278 errors). Error trials were decoded with decoders fitted on all correct trials of the same cue pair, so they were also out of sample, and their rates were $z$-scored with the mean and SD of correct trials. For each trial, we computed the total Off time and the fraction of time On in the delay (500--1,400~ms after cue onset), the number of Off states and their mean length (13,962 trials with at least one Off state). For each statistic, the probability of an error was modelled with a logistic mixed model, $\text{error} \sim z(\text{stat}) + t + t^2 + \text{monkey} + (1 + z(\text{stat}) \,\|\, \text{session})$, where $z(\text{stat})$ is the statistic $z$-scored within each session, so that only within-session variation is used, $t$ is the time within the session, and each session has a random intercept and an uncorrelated random slope. Monkey was a fixed effect, as three monkeys are too few to estimate a random effect. Models were fitted with the Laplace approximation and tested with likelihood-ratio tests against the same model without the statistic. Fitted to each monkey separately, the odds ratio of an error per SD of total Off time was 1.11 ($p = 0.13$), 1.61 ($p = 1\times10^{-4}$) and 1.68 ($p = 0.005$) in monkeys A, H and J. In Fig.~3b, trials were binned by Off time in 91~ms bins (one tenth of the delay window), and fraction correct was computed for each session in bins with at least 5 trials.

\subsection{Fitting}

Six parameters were fitted with the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy \citep[CMA-ES;][]{hansen2001} to the decoder using all neurons: $\bm{w_s}$, $\bm{\lambda_d}$, $\bm{\mu_d}$, $\bm{k}$, $\bm{\delta}$ and $\bm{\sigma}$.
The NMDA decay time was not fitted but fixed at $\tau_N = 150$~ms, within the range measured in cortex \citep{vicini1998,wangm2013}.
The loss combined the squared scaled errors of six statistics (On and Off states per trial, mean lengths and fractions of the delay), the squared Hellinger distance between model and data length distributions (states $\geq 40$~ms), and a penalty when the memory was correct in fewer than 98\% of trials.
Best fit: $\bm{w_s} = 1.30$, $\bm{\lambda_d} = 10.2$/s, $\bm{\mu_d} = 90$~ms, $\bm{k} = 8.7$, $\bm{\delta} = 0.60$, $\bm{\sigma} = 0.006$.

\subsection{Decoding read-out, On/Off labelling and statistics}

The decoder $z$ score was modelled as $z(t) = c\, \Delta r(t) + \eta(t)$, where $\Delta r$ is the cued minus uncued rate in 100~ms windows stepped by 10~ms.
The noise $\eta$ has unit variance, the autocorrelation of overlapping windows, and a slow per-trial offset whose variance share was measured from the pre-cue $z$ of the data (0.35 for all neurons, 0.09 for the most selective neurons, 0.11 for random neurons).
The gain $c$ was not fitted: for each decoded population, it was chosen so that the upper mean of a two-component Gaussian mixture of delay $z$ values matched the data's.
We call this upper mean the decoding strength.
States were labelled as in \citet{panichello2024}.
On states are runs of $z > 1.96$ whose summed $z$ exceeds the 95th percentile of the same quantity in pure noise.
Off states are runs of at least 5 points (50~ms) with $z < 0.84$.
States were counted from 0 to 1,400~ms after cue onset, and fractions of the delay spent On or Off from 500 to 1,400~ms.
Model statistics were computed from 2,112 simulated trials (792 per evaluation during fitting).
Data statistics are means over the 25 sessions; for random neurons, the 10 draws were first averaged within each session.
